System of Eternity (SOE)
Civilizational Governance Framework
A Non-Terminal Governance Architecture for Long-Term Civilization
Executive Summary
Modern civilization faces a long-standing structural challenge: political and institutional systems often become rigid or lose stability over time.
Many historical institutional collapses did not occur because their original designs were entirely flawed. Rather, they failed because they could not adapt to:
technological progress
changes in social structures
the continuous expansion of civilizational scale
The System of Eternity (SOE) proposes a different governance approach.
It does not attempt to design a “final correct” political system. Instead, it proposes a non-terminal institutional framework — a civilizational governance structure capable of:
continuous self-adjustment
ongoing institutional upgrades
peaceful replacement by superior systems when necessary
Core Principles
SOE is built upon the following core principles:
Non-Terminal Principle No institutional system should claim to represent the final form of civilization.
Upgrade Principle Governance structures must allow gradual and continuous improvement.
Replaceability Principle When superior institutional arrangements emerge, peaceful system replacement must be possible.
Right of Exit Individuals and groups must possess legitimate pathways to exit institutional arrangements.
Continuous Oversight Principle All power structures must remain subject to ongoing oversight.
Civilizational Structure
SOE does not organize civilization as a single centralized nation-state system.
Instead, it proposes a new structure:
Node Civilization Network
Within this structure, different nodes — such as cities, regions, or even future space settlements — may adopt different governance models while maintaining cooperation and coordination through shared Civilizational Protocols.
This structure enables:
institutional experimentation
peaceful coexistence of multiple governance systems
institutional evolution through practical experience
Core Objective
The System of Eternity does not seek to discover a final political system.
Its objective is to ensure that civilization always retains the capacity to continuously improve its institutions without destroying the overall governance structure.
Therefore, the System of Eternity (SOE) is a non-terminal civilizational governance framework.
Its purpose is to maintain long-term stability while enabling institutions to:
continuously evolve
continuously upgrade
and be peacefully replaced when superior systems emerge.
Preface
Throughout history, human civilization has demonstrated remarkable creativity, cooperative capacity, and technological progress. At the same time, history has repeatedly revealed a number of long-term structural challenges faced by civilizations, including institutional rigidity, ideological conflict, concentration of power, technological risks, and the potential for systemic collapse after long periods of institutional operation.
Most governance systems were established under relatively limited historical conditions. They often assumed that technological environments, social structures, and political boundaries would remain relatively stable over extended periods of time. However, the future development of human civilization may unfold across time scales far longer than those previously experienced, and may involve profound changes in technological conditions, social organization, and the scale of civilization itself.
With the advancement of technology, the emergence of artificial intelligence, and the possible expansion of human activity beyond Earth, future civilizations may encounter new forms of social organization, new types of intelligent entities, and even new modes of existence. In such a context, governance systems designed for a particular historical stage may struggle to adapt to the long-term development of civilization.
The System of Eternity (SOE) is not intended to design a final political system. Instead, it proposes a governance framework oriented toward the long-term development of civilization, with the goal of maintaining institutional adaptability and evolvability across extremely long time scales.
This framework does not attempt to eliminate disagreement, nor does it seek to enforce uniform values. Rather, it aims to establish an institutional structure that can transform potentially destructive conflicts into processes of structured negotiation, experimentation, and continuous evolution.
Within this framework, a fundamental premise is that:
Civilizations endure not because they achieve complete agreement, but because they are capable of maintaining stable operation despite persistent differences.
For this reason, the System of Eternity emphasizes distributed governance structures, institutional experimentation under node autonomy, mechanisms for system upgrade and system replacement, and the long-term preservation of civilizational knowledge. Together, these mechanisms form an institutional structure that allows civilization to continuously evolve, rather than becoming fixed within a single permanent institutional form.
Beyond governance structures and institutional stability, this framework is also grounded in a deeper understanding: civilization is not merely an organizational structure for resources and power. It is also a space in which different forms of life, intelligence, creativity, and imagination can exist and develop.
As civilization evolves, new ideologies and new forms of existence may emerge—whether biological, digital, or forms not yet imagined. A stable and adaptable civilizational system should be capable of providing a safe environment in which these diverse forms of existence can coexist and develop.
The System of Eternity is proposed as an exploration within this broader context.
It is not presented as a final answer, nor as an institutional model that must be universally adopted. Rather, it can be understood as a conceptual architecture—an institutional vision of how civilization might remain stable, continuously adapt, and preserve an open future across centuries or even millennia of change.
If such a system were to succeed, its significance would not lie in creating a permanent and fixed institution, but in enabling civilization to retain the capacity to continuously redesign itself.
In this sense, civilization endures over the long term not because it discovers a perfect system, but because it learns how to continuously improve itself.

Part 0 — Civilizational Foundations
0.1 The Structural Roots of Political Conflict
For most of human history, political conflict has been strongly influenced by material scarcity.
When key resources such as land, food, housing, energy, and healthcare exist in limited supply, societies must determine how these resources are allocated. These allocation decisions often create competition among individuals, social groups, and institutions.
Many long-standing political debates—including those surrounding economic inequality, healthcare access, housing availability, and immigration—can be understood as structural outcomes of limited resources.
Different political ideologies often propose different ways to manage these constraints. However, the underlying condition of scarcity itself has rarely changed.
As a result, many political systems primarily focus on managing conflict, rather than addressing the structural conditions that generate those conflicts.
0.2 The Abundance Transition
Technological development may significantly alter the conditions that have long shaped political conflict.
Advances in automation, artificial intelligence, advanced manufacturing, and energy production are rapidly increasing humanity’s ability to produce goods and services.
At the same time, the expansion of human activity into space may provide access to vastly greater material resources and habitable environments.
These developments suggest a possible future condition:
Production capacity may eventually exceed the scale of resources required for basic human survival.
Under such circumstances, many basic resources—including food production, housing construction, energy supply, water systems, and medical infrastructure—may increasingly function as civilizational infrastructure rather than scarce commodities.
This transformation would not eliminate all limitations, but it would fundamentally change the structural relationship between scarcity and survival.
0.3 Decoupling Survival from Currency
In most contemporary societies, survival is closely tied to economic participation.
Individuals typically obtain basic resources through income, creating a direct relationship between employment, money, and basic survival needs.
Under conditions of technological abundance, this relationship may gradually weaken.
When production capacity significantly exceeds survival needs, access to basic resources can increasingly be provided through infrastructure systems rather than solely through market allocation.
Within such a framework, basic necessities—such as food, housing, water, energy, and basic healthcare—may no longer depend primarily on individual income levels.
Monetary systems may still exist in many areas of exchange, but they would no longer determine whether individuals are able to meet their fundamental survival needs.
This implies a structural transformation in economic organization.
Historically, economic systems have primarily relied on two models:
Market allocation mechanisms
Centralized planning mechanisms
However, technological development may enable a third form of organization:
In this model, the key resources required for human survival increasingly function as civilizational infrastructure.
Within such a system, individuals’ ability to obtain basic resources would no longer depend primarily on purchasing power, but rather on the stable operation of large-scale production systems supported by automation and advanced technology.
0.4 Voluntary Civilizational Contribution
When survival is no longer directly dependent on income, the way individuals participate in economic and public life may also change.
Individuals may increasingly contribute to society through voluntary participation in activities such as:
scientific research
engineering and technological development
governance and public administration
cultural creation
education
exploration
Automation and advanced technological systems may perform most of the material production necessary to sustain civilization.
As a result, the motivations for human participation in social activity may shift away from survival pressure and instead be driven more by:
curiosity
creativity
civic identity
social recognition
Historically, some societies organized public contributions through mandatory systems. For example, the Mit'a system required citizens to periodically participate in labor for public works or state projects.
In a highly technological civilization, similar forms of social contribution might emerge on a voluntary basis, supported by automation and resource abundance rather than enforced labor.
This transformation also implies a structural change in the role of money within civilization.
Although exchange systems may continue to exist for certain specialized goods and services, basic survival resources may increasingly function as civilizational infrastructure rather than commodities.
Within such a system, individuals’ access to essential resources would no longer be primarily determined by purchasing power.
Instead, human survival would depend on shared infrastructure systems maintained through technological production and voluntary social contribution.
0.5 Persistent Value Conflicts
Even if material scarcity were significantly reduced, human societies would still differ in areas such as:
cultural values
governance preferences
ethical principles
attitudes toward technology
Conflicts surrounding issues such as individual freedom, collective security, technological regulation, cultural traditions, and social organization do not arise solely from economic conditions.
These differences may continue to exist even in conditions of material abundance.
Therefore, future civilizations will still require governance systems capable of accommodating diverse social models while maintaining stable cooperation.
The System of Eternity (SOE) is proposed within this context as a governance architecture designed to address these challenges in a highly technological and highly diverse civilization.
0.6 Structural Scarcity Decoupling Mechanism
In traditional societies, large-scale social and political conflicts are often driven by material scarcity and the dependence of survival on specific economic and geographic conditions.
When access to basic survival resources—such as food, housing, healthcare, and energy—is limited or unevenly distributed, individuals and groups may be compelled into competition. This dynamic may manifest in:
resource allocation conflicts
economic inequality
large-scale migration pressures
social and political instability
In such contexts, many political conflicts are not solely the result of value differences, but are instead driven by structural pressures associated with survival constraints.
Mechanism Definition
The System of Eternity reduces these pressures through two long-term structural transformations:
1. Resource Abundance
Advances in automation, artificial intelligence, and production technologies may allow basic survival resources to function increasingly as civilizational infrastructure rather than scarce commodities.
2. Decoupling Survival from Economic Constraints
Access to essential resources may no longer depend primarily on income or market participation, but instead on stable infrastructure systems.
Structural Outcomes
Under these conditions:
human behavior shifts from necessity-driven to preference-driven
large-scale migration shifts from survival-driven to voluntary mobility
social conflict shifts from zero-sum competition to problems of coordination and institutional design
System-Level Significance
This mechanism does not eliminate all forms of competition or disagreement. Instead, it reduces forced competition arising from survival pressure, allowing most conflicts to be addressed through institutional coordination rather than resource struggle.
This transformation forms one of the foundational conditions for long-term civilizational stability within the System of Eternity.
Layer II — System Architecture

Part I — Foundations of the System of Eternity
Chapter 1 — Problem Statement
For most of human history, governance systems have primarily been designed to manage conflict and allocate resources.
States, legal institutions, and political organizations have typically been structured around the following questions:
How should limited resources be distributed?
How should competition among different groups be regulated?
How can social order and political stability be maintained?
Under conditions of resource scarcity, these institutional arrangements have played an essential role.
However, with continued technological advancement and increasing productive capacity, civilization may be gradually entering a new historical stage. In this stage, the material resources required to sustain basic human survival may no longer constitute the primary limiting factor.
When production capacity significantly exceeds survival needs, many of the central issues around which traditional political systems are organized may begin to change.
Under such conditions, political systems may no longer focus primarily on resource allocation, but instead confront a different set of challenges:
How to maintain stability among highly diverse social value systems
How to coordinate the coexistence of different governance models
How to prevent long-term entrenchment of power structures
How to enable institutional evolution without undermining social stability
Most existing political institutions were not designed to address these long-term challenges.
Many systems were originally established under the assumption that they would remain stable—or even permanent—over time. As a result, when historical conditions change, these systems often lack effective mechanisms for self-renewal.
This leads to a fundamental question:
Can human civilization design a governance structure that preserves stability while also allowing institutions to continuously evolve over long time scales?
The System of Eternity (SOE) is proposed as a governance framework developed in response to this question.
The objective of SOE is not to establish a final political system. Rather, it seeks to design a governance architecture that allows institutions to be continuously improved, upgraded, and replaced when necessary.
Through such a framework, civilization may be able to maintain basic stability while gradually adapting to long-term changes in technology, social structures, and the diversity of human values.
Constitution of the System of Eternity (SOE)
Preamble
The System of Eternity seeks to maintain a civilizational framework capable of sustaining long-term existence. Its purpose is to enable civilization to preserve stability and continuity amid technological transformation, ideological divergence, and ongoing civilizational evolution.
The system does not attempt to eliminate conflict.
Instead, it establishes structural mechanisms that transform potentially destructive conflicts into negotiated compromises. Through this process, different forms of intelligence may coexist without causing the collapse of the civilizational system itself.
Article I — The Axiom of Existence
The primary constraint of the System of Eternity is the preservation of the continued existence of the civilizational network.
No entity, node, or optimization process may undermine the ability of the civilizational network to accommodate multiple value systems and civilizational forms.
Any attempt to carry out civilizational deletion or actions that could trigger irreversible systemic collapse shall activate structural containment mechanisms.
Article II — The Compromise Principle
The survival of the system depends upon the continued operation of compromise mechanisms.
All nodes must maintain the structural capacity, cognitive capability, and diplomatic competence necessary to participate in the civilizational compromise process.
Any entity that permanently abandons the capacity for compromise shall be regarded as operating outside the protected network.
Article III — Pluralism Within Structural Constraints
The system does not impose a unified value system.
Individual nodes may pursue different:
cultural trajectories
technological trajectories
biological evolutionary pathways
digital evolutionary pathways
However, such freedom must operate within the following structural constraints:
the Axiom of Existence
the Compromise Mechanism
the autonomy of other nodes
Article IV — Civilizational Interoperability
All participating nodes must maintain interoperable communication and negotiation protocols.
These protocols enable interaction among different forms of intelligence, including:
biological intelligence
digital intelligence
hybrid intelligence
and future evolutionary forms that may emerge
The loss of interoperability constitutes an isolation risk and may trigger containment procedures.
Article V — Heritage Preservation Principle
Baseline humans and their cultural traditions are regarded as foundational components of the civilizational system.
The system shall ensure that baseline human societies continue to function as active participants within the network through protected environments, institutional support, and incentive mechanisms.
Participation remains voluntary, but the continuation of human civilization is considered a civilizational-level priority.
Article V-A — The Foundational Share
Baseline humanity possesses a Foundational Share within the governance architecture.
This share grants baseline human nodes an asymmetric veto over constitutional modifications concerning:
the Axiom of Existence
the Compromise Mechanism
the structural survival of baseline human civilization
This mechanism ensures that humans remain sovereign participants within the system rather than being maintained merely as a “cultural heritage population.”
Article VI — Evolutionary Freedom
Individuals and nodes may pursue different forms of evolutionary development, including:
biological enhancement
digital consciousness
hybrid forms of existence
other evolutionary pathways that may emerge
However, such evolutionary developments must not:
eliminate the autonomy of other nodes
violate interoperability protocols
damage protected civilizational heritage zones
The system encourages evolutionary diversity, as it constitutes one of the sources of civilizational resilience.
Article VII — Structural Enforcement
The system enforces its rules through structural constraints rather than moral judgment.
It does not evaluate internal value systems but will respond to actions that threaten:
the survival of civilization
the compromise mechanism
the autonomy of other participants
When necessary, the system may initiate:
containment measures
isolation procedures
defensive responses
Article VIII — Civilizational Stability Through Negotiation
The System of Eternity assumes that disagreements among participants will persist.
The system does not seek complete harmony. Instead, it transforms conflict into structured negotiation through institutional design.
The long-term persistence of civilization depends upon the continuous operation of this compromise mechanism.

democratic systems

market-based systems

international conflicts
geopolitical rivalry
institutional instability

freedom
security
democracy
stability
innovation
social equity

freedom vs. security
efficiency vs. fairness
democracy vs. stability

Modern civilization is entering an era of accelerating technological development.
Key emerging technologies include:
artificial intelligence
biotechnology
automated warfare systems
large-scale information manipulation technologies
These technologies carry potential civilizational-scale risks.

nation-state level governance scope
short-term decision cycles
limited long-term coordination mechanisms
As a result, technological development may outpace the capacity of governance systems to manage its risks.

Future human civilization may expand beyond Earth to include:
space habitats
the Moon
Mars
the asteroid belt
Traditional nation-state governance structures may not easily adapt to such multi-planetary conditions.
New civilizational scales will require new governance architectures.
5. Institutional Rigidity
Historically, once institutions are established, they often become difficult to change.
Institutions tend to:
protect their existing structures
resist reform
prevent replacement
This phenomenon can lead to long-term civilizational stagnation and may ultimately force institutional change through severe conflict.

continuously evolve

long-term stability

Chapter 2 — Core Principles
This chapter establishes the constitutional-level foundations of the System of Eternity (SOE).
All subsequent institutional designs, node structures, and governance mechanisms must adhere to these principles.
2.1 Role of the Core Principles
One major reason why civilizational institutions fail is the absence of stable foundational principles.
When political environments change, technologies advance, or social conflicts emerge, institutions may:
lose stability
become rigid
or fall under the control of extreme value systems
For this reason, SOE requires a limited number of highly stable core principles.
These principles must:
remain valid across long time scales
not depend on any specific political system
remain applicable to future civilizations operating at larger scales
Together, these principles form the fundamental constraint framework of the System of Eternity.
2.2 First Principle
The Compromise Mechanism Must Not Be Eliminated
Human civilization simultaneously contains multiple important values, including:
freedom
security
democracy
stability
innovation
social equity
These values often exist in natural tension with one another.
For example:
freedom vs. security
efficiency vs. fairness
democratic decision-making vs. long-term stability
Throughout history, many institutional systems have attempted to elevate one value to absolute dominance, such as:
extreme libertarianism
extreme central authoritarianism
purely technocratic governance
Over time, such systems often lose balance.
Therefore, the first principle of SOE is:
Civilization must preserve a mechanism that allows continuous compromise among competing values.
No institutional system or political force may permanently eliminate this compromise mechanism.
If any single value completely overwhelms the others, systemic stability will gradually deteriorate.
2.3 Second Principle
The System Must Be Upgradable
Civilization is not static.
Over time, new conditions continually emerge, including:
new technologies
new social structures
new civilizational scales
new ethical challenges
An institutional system that cannot evolve will eventually become rigid and lose its ability to respond to emerging problems.
Historically, many institutional collapses occurred not because their principles were incorrect, but because their structures lacked mechanisms for adaptation.
Therefore, the System of Eternity must maintain the capacity for continuous upgrade.
This means that:
institutional structures can be modified
governance methods can be improved
new institutional mechanisms can be introduced
However, any upgrade process must remain:
transparent
stable
and subject to systematic evaluation.
2.4 Third Principle
The System Must Allow Replacement
SOE does not claim to be a final institutional system.
Any civilizational system may eventually be replaced by a superior structure.
If a system attempts to permanently preserve its own existence, it may ultimately become:
a rigid institutional structure
a self-protecting power system
a barrier to civilizational progress
Therefore, the third principle of SOE is:
The system must allow itself to be replaced by a superior system.
If a future governance architecture proves to be more stable, more effective, and more aligned with the long-term interests of civilization, SOE must permit that transition.
The goal of civilization is not to protect a particular institutional system, but to preserve the capacity for continued civilizational development.
2.5 Relationship Between the Three Principles
The three core principles of SOE form a mutually balancing structure:
Compromise Mechanism ↓ System Upgrade ↓ System Replacement
Together, they ensure that:
civilization will not be dominated by a single value system
institutions can continuously evolve
governance structures do not become permanently fixed
These three principles form the constitutional foundation layer of the System of Eternity.
All subsequent governance structures must operate within these constraints.
2.6 Constitutional Stability Layer
The System of Eternity explicitly distinguishes between:
constitutional principles
and
operational governance structures.
Operational mechanisms—such as:
governance procedures
decision systems
institutional structures
are designed to evolve continuously through the System Upgrade mechanism.
Core principles, however, serve a different function.
They form the Constitutional Stability Layer of the civilizational architecture.
These principles establish the fundamental constraints that all governance systems must respect.
Because the System of Eternity encourages continuous experimentation and institutional evolution, a potential risk exists:
civilizational drift
This refers to the possibility that, through many small institutional upgrades over time, the foundational values of the system may gradually shift.
To prevent such drift, the core principles cannot be modified through ordinary system upgrades.
Any modification of these principles must occur through the System Replacement mechanism.
This process requires:
a higher degree of civilizational consensus
long-term institutional validation
This layered design ensures that:
governance mechanisms remain adaptable
institutional experimentation remains possible
civilizational foundational principles remain stable over long time scales
Through this distinction, the System of Eternity preserves both:
the flexibility of institutional evolution
and the continuity of constitutional foundations.

Cooperation

a multi-node social structure

planetary civilizations

large-scale space societies.

democratic majority rule

experts

Long-term civilizational stability depends not only on material infrastructure but also on the integrity and credibility of shared knowledge systems.

To address this risk, SOE establishes an Epistemic Governance Protocol to ensure diversity, transparency, and long-term reliability in civilizational knowledge systems.

Civilizational knowledge systems must be constructed from multiple independent sources, including:
academic research institutions
independent research organizations
distributed scientific collaboration networks
artificial intelligence analytical systems
public data archives
cross-node research alliances

25A.2 Knowledge Version Preservation

original research data
alternative theoretical interpretations
historical revision records
minority and dissenting viewpoints
By preserving multiple interpretive pathways, civilization avoids long-term cognitive lock-in and maintains openness in its knowledge system.

Major updates to civilizational knowledge must undergo distributed verification processes.
Participants may include:
expert review institutions
citizen knowledge panels
independent AI verification systems
cross-node scientific collaboration networks

continuously challenging mainstream knowledge assumptions

evaluating cognitive risks in civilizational governance
The Epistemic Red Team strengthens the long-term resilience of civilizational knowledge systems.

To prevent epistemic governance structures from becoming formalized power centers or being controlled by specific groups, SOE establishes several safeguards:

citizen review panels

Chapter 26 — System Capture Prevention
26.1 The Problem
Historically, many institutions have not collapsed because their designs were fundamentally flawed, but because they were gradually captured by particular groups.
Examples include:
political elites maintaining long-term control of power
capital groups exerting strong influence over policy
information manipulation shaping public perception
When such conditions persist over long periods, institutions may gradually deviate from their original objectives.
Therefore, the System of Eternity (SOE) must incorporate mechanisms designed to prevent system capture.
26.2 Principle of Power Distribution
The first line of defense against system capture is distribution of power.
Power is not concentrated in a single institution but distributed among multiple actors, including:
citizen participation mechanisms
expert systems
institutional bodies
AI analytical systems
These participants collectively form a multi-layer system of checks and balances.
26.3 Node Autonomy
Node autonomy is another important mechanism preventing system-wide capture.
If the institutions of a particular node become controlled by a specific group, other nodes can still:
maintain independent institutional structures
conduct institutional experiments
sustain alternative governance systems
This structure prevents the entire civilizational system from falling under a single concentration of power.
26.4 Information Transparency
Information transparency reduces the risk of institutional manipulation.
Examples include:
publicly recorded policies
open data access
decision explanation mechanisms
Transparent institutions strengthen social oversight capacity.
26.5 Multi-layer Review
SOE establishes a multi-layer review system, including:
judicial review
expert evaluation
citizen oversight
AI risk analysis
If abnormal behavior appears within certain power structures, other actors can identify and respond to the issue promptly.
26.6 Institutional Adaptability
Institutional adaptability is another key safeguard against system capture.
If certain institutional structures become persistently manipulated, the civilizational system can respond through:
institutional reform
node-based experimentation
system upgrades
These tools allow governance structures to adjust over time.
26.7 Objectives of System Capture Prevention
The objective of System Capture Prevention is:
to prevent any single group from exercising long-term control over the civilizational system.
SOE reduces the risk of capture through:
distribution of power
node autonomy
information transparency
institutional upgrade mechanisms
26.8 Long-Term System Dynamics Considerations
The System of Eternity (SOE) is designed as a non-terminal governance framework capable of evolving and adapting over extremely long time scales.
Because the system may span hundreds or even thousands of years of civilizational development, certain long-term structural dynamics may gradually emerge that cannot be fully predicted during the initial design phase.
The following potential dynamics are listed for future system research and upgrades.
1. Epistemic Authority Drift
Over time, distributed knowledge systems may gradually form structures of epistemic authority.
Nodes with stronger technological or research capabilities may gradually gain greater influence over:
knowledge production
knowledge storage
knowledge interpretation
SOE reduces this risk through mechanisms such as:
the Human Knowledge Vault
the Knowledge Audit Chain
multi-layer knowledge verification systems
However, over extremely long time scales, concentration of epistemic authority may still occur. Future system upgrades must continue monitoring this risk.

Node civilizations are allowed to conduct extensive experimentation in areas such as:
institutional design
technological systems
cultural structures
Over time, different nodes may develop significantly different:
technological frameworks
social structures
value systems
governance models

3. Institutional Complexity Growth
As civilization develops, governance systems tend to accumulate new institutions and rules.
Without adjustment mechanisms, this growing complexity may lead to:
declining decision efficiency
reduced institutional transparency
decreased citizen participation
SOE reduces this risk through mechanisms such as:
periodic system upgrades

node-level institutional experimentation
These mechanisms are designed to maintain long-term institutional adaptability.

Appendix D — SOE Versioning Protocol
D.1 Purpose
The System of Eternity (SOE) is an open institutional framework designed for the long-term evolution of civilization.
Because civilizational environments, technological conditions, and social structures continuously change, SOE cannot be finalized in a single design phase.
Therefore, the system adopts a Versioned Evolution Model, allowing institutional structures to improve through continuous auditing and iterative refinement.
The objectives of the Versioning Protocol include:
preventing the design process from falling into endless discussion loops
providing a stable rhythm for institutional improvement
ensuring the system gradually improves through continuous auditing
D.2 Version Levels
SOE adopts a three-tier version structure.
1. Major Version
A Major Version represents significant changes to the civilizational architecture, such as:
introduction of new governance layers
modification of core principles
major restructuring of the resource system
Examples:
SOE v1.0
SOE v2.0
Major versions typically require long-term auditing and broad civilizational discussion.
2. Minor Version
A Minor Version records structural expansions after the architecture has stabilized, such as:
new governance protocols
additional institutional modules
new security mechanisms
Examples:
SOE v0.8
SOE v0.9
SOE v1.1
Minor versions represent the most common form of system updates.
3. Patch Version
A Patch Version addresses implementation-level issues, such as:
improvements to protocol details
security patches
optimization of operational rules
Examples:
SOE v0.8.1
SOE v0.8.2
These updates do not alter the system’s architectural structure.
D.3 Architectural Stability Criteria
SOE may be considered an architecturally stable version when the following conditions are met:
Red Team audits cannot identify architecture-level fatal flaws
all major criticisms can be resolved through institutional patches
core principles remain consistent
When these conditions are satisfied, the system may enter a stable iteration phase.
D.4 Audit Mechanisms
SOE encourages continuous Adversarial Auditing.
Audits typically include:
architecture-level attack simulations
institutional abuse testing
extreme scenario simulations
If audits reveal new architectural problems, a new Minor Version should be released.
If issues involve only implementation details, a Patch Version should be released.
D.5 Release Cycle
SOE follows the cycle:
Design → Audit → Revision → Release
At the end of each stage, a new draft may be published.
This mechanism prevents the institutional design process from becoming indefinitely delayed.
D.6 Principle of Perpetual Auditing
The design philosophy of SOE assumes that:
no long-term civilizational system can ever be completely free of vulnerabilities.
Therefore:
auditing should remain permanent
criticism should not be interpreted as system failure
continuous revision is a natural part of civilizational evolution
The versioning system allows the framework to maintain stability while continuously improving over time.
