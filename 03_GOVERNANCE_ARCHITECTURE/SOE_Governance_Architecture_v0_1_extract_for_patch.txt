P000: System of Eternity Governance Architecture
P001: Draft v0.1 - Architecture Design v2 synthesis - 2026-05-07
P002: Status note: This is a governance-architecture synthesis draft, not the arXiv simulation paper. It consolidates the current C00-C08 Architecture Design v2 specification set with the v18, v19, and v19b simulation findings. Claims are stated as simulation-constrained architecture claims unless separately marked as open, assumed, or deployment-calibration required.
P003: Abstract
P004: The System of Eternity (SOE) Governance Architecture is a nine-component institutional design framework for maintaining trust, filtering disturbance, classifying stability, recovering degraded nodes, and constraining governance itself. The architecture treats trust T(t), governance capacity G(t), disturbance D(t), identity support I(t), stability S(t), and meta-governance stress Psi(t) as coupled variables in a dynamic network. The core thesis is that resilient governance cannot be designed as a single decision body, central hub, or static constitution; it must be designed as a layered control architecture with topology-aware monitoring, baseline recovery, asynchronous coordination, and non-unilateral meta-governance constraints.
P005: Architecture Design v2 converts prior SOE simulation results into institutional mechanisms. The current version locks several design constraints: star topology is disallowed under SOE deployment assumptions; exactly two hubs are insufficient for hub-redundant star repair; three hubs are the minimum safe hub-redundancy condition in tested configurations; federation topology contains local stress under tested disturbance but cannot be monitored through the same S-network threshold used for ring, mesh, and star structures; and federation requires Psi_extended with a cluster-variance term as its primary stress detector. Open items remain, especially calibration of recovery gain k_b, governance coefficients, Psi weights, class-specific identity weights, and real-world measurement proxies.
P006: 1. Purpose and Scope
P007: This paper is the governance-architecture paper for SOE. Its purpose is to state the architecture as an integrated mechanism: what the components are, how they depend on one another, what the simulations currently constrain, and what must be calibrated before deployment. It is not a proof of universal governance safety and it is not an empirical claim about any existing state, company, institution, or AI system.
P008: The paper should be read as the bridge between component specifications and the future grand simulation. Component specs define local behavior; this paper defines the system-level architecture that the grand simulation should test.
P009: 2. Architecture Thesis
P010: SOE begins from a simple structural claim: governance failure is often not caused by one missing rule. It emerges when trust decline, disturbance exposure, governance decay, identity fragmentation, and coordination delay reinforce each other. A governance architecture therefore needs separate layers for measuring trust, filtering shocks, classifying stability, restoring trust, maintaining structural identity, governing ordinary operations, constraining governance itself, and coordinating asynchronous updates.
P011: The central design objective is not to make collapse impossible. The objective is to prevent hidden collapse, localize failure when it occurs, preserve recovery pathways, and ensure that no governance layer can become the unchecked final authority over its own constraints.
P012: 3. Component Stack
P014: 4. Core Dynamics
P015: 4.1 Trust Dynamics
P016: The trust layer maintains per-node trust T_i(t). A compact architecture-level form is:
P017: dT/dt = -aD_effective + bI - cC + R(T,t)
P018: D_effective is filtered disturbance, I is structural identity support, C is cognitive distortion or coordination-cost pressure depending on the component context, and R(T,t) is active recovery. The architecture requires T(t) to be observable, continuous, node-local, directional, asymmetrically sensitive to decline, and network-scoped.
P019: 4.2 Governance Dynamics
P020: Governance capacity G(t) evolves as:
P021: dG/dt = gamma*T - delta*G + H(t)
P022: G(t) is downstream of trust: governance can remain formally present while effective capacity collapses. This is why the architecture separates governance existence from governance effectiveness as an explicit unresolved modeling issue. H(t) represents temporary Hero/Witness activation and is treated as emergency amplification, not routine governance.
P023: 4.3 Disturbance Filtering
P024: The Disturbance Buffer intercepts raw stress before it reaches trust dynamics. Its core coupling relation is:
P025: k_D_effective = k_D * (1 - alpha_gov * G(t)), floor: f_min = 0.30
P026: The floor f_min = 0.30 is treated as an empirical operating constraint in the tested regimes. It prevents governance from suppressing disturbance so completely that the system loses signal fidelity or hides collapse. It is not a universal physical constant.
P027: 4.4 Recovery Dynamics
P028: The Recovery Engine uses four additive pathways: R_internal, R_network, R_gov, and R_base. R_internal works when trust is already high enough for self-correction. R_network transfers support from healthy peers but is constrained by T_reserve so donor nodes are not depleted. R_gov indirectly reduces trust erosion through governance suppression. R_base is the unconditional deadlock breaker.
P029: The v2.1 break condition for the trust-governance feedback loop is:
P030: k_b * (1 + gamma_D * f_min) > a * f_min * T_floor + delta * G_min
P031: Here gamma_D is the disturbance-adaptive recovery gain. This is a sufficient simulation-level calibration condition for breaking the modeled T-G loop under floor conditions, not a proof that R_base can defeat unlimited sustained external disturbance.
P032: 5. Topology as a Governance Variable
P033: SOE treats topology as load-bearing governance structure. The same rules cannot be safely applied to star, mesh, ring, and federation networks because stress propagates and becomes observable through different paths. A deployment must classify topology before selecting monitoring, buffering, and trigger logic.
P035: 5.1 Federation Monitoring Finding
P036: The v19b result changes how federation should be described. Federation does not merely require a recalibrated S_threshold. The S_network threshold form of Trigger A is not applicable to federation because the same two-tier aggregation that keeps cluster stress local also prevents enough network-level S signal from accumulating. This is an architectural property, not a calibration failure.
P037: For federation topology, Trigger A must be delegated to C07 Psi_extended:
P038: Psi_extended = Psi_base + lambda * sum_k (N_k/N) * Var(T_i in C_k), with lambda = 0.1
P039: C04 S_threshold is therefore closed as a federation question. Federation stress detection belongs to C07.
P040: 6. Meta Governance
P041: Meta Governance answers the question: who constrains the governance system itself? In SOE, that answer cannot be a single body, because a single final authority can capture the architecture. C07 therefore operates as a constraint layer above ordinary governance. It does not produce trust, distribute resources, or perform day-to-day coordination. It monitors structural stress, activates topology-conditioned triggers, and enforces meta rules that cannot be suspended by lower governance layers.
P043: The permanent meta rules are falsifiability, non-unilateral trigger authority, upgrade-preserves-constraints, rollback, and non-terminality. Together they ensure that governance can be repaired without allowing the repair mechanism to become absolute.
P044: 7. Heterogeneous Nodes and Structural Support
P045: Governance networks are mixed: human agents, formal institutions, and AI systems may all participate. C05 v2.1 therefore separates node classes so that psychological belonging is not incorrectly assigned to non-human entities.
P047: The v19 SSL simulation supports the qualitative timescale assertion: trust-memory recovery was approximately 2.1-2.33 times slower than cooperation recovery across 20 runs. This supports the idea that structural trust memory moves more slowly than operational coordination, but it does not yet calibrate real-world weeks, months, or years.
P048: 8. Simulation-Constrained Findings
P050: 9. Deployment Protocol
P051: A real SOE deployment should not begin by choosing a governance body. It should begin by mapping the network and declaring the measurement rules. A minimal deployment protocol is:
P052: Declare node set and node class for every participant: Class H, Class I, or Class A. Unclassified nodes default to Class I to avoid false psychological attribution.
P053: Classify topology: ring, mesh, star, federation, hub-redundant, or unknown. Unknown topology uses conservative monitoring defaults until classified.
P054: Select topology-conditioned monitoring: C04 S_network threshold for ring/mesh/star/unknown; C07 Psi_extended for federation.
P055: Set recovery calibration: choose k_b, gamma_D, beta, and floor conditions so the C02 break condition is satisfied where claimed.
P056: Declare governance effectiveness proxy separately from governance existence, or explicitly state that G(t) still conflates the two.
P057: Run pre-deployment stress simulation using the exact node set, topology, update intervals, disturbance model, and trigger rules.
P058: Create rollback paths for every trigger activation before deployment. No trigger should be open-ended.
P059: 10. Open Calibration and Architecture Risks
P061: The most important architecture risk is false stability: a monitoring method can report stability while a local cluster, peripheral set, or hidden governance dependency is failing. The star topology and federation findings are both examples of this risk in different directions. Star hides hub-centered fragility; federation hides cluster-internal stress from S_network threshold detection.
P062: 11. Grand Simulation Starting Point
P063: The next grand simulation should test SOE as an integrated architecture rather than as isolated component checks. It should include mixed node classes, topology changes over time, asynchronous update intervals, governance delay/budget limits, disturbance regimes, trigger activation latency, and recovery resource depletion.
P064: Minimum grand-sim scenarios should include:
P065: Federation with Psi_extended monitoring under local cluster attack, bridge-node stress, and cross-cluster disturbance migration.
P066: Hub-redundant structures with 2, 3, and 4 hubs, including hub imbalance and delayed hub failure.
P067: Mixed Class H/I/A networks with institutional legitimacy loss, AI role-only nodes, and Class I proxy degradation.
P068: Governance effectiveness collapse where G(t) remains formally present but operationally captured or ineffective.
P069: Trigger B fallback under sustained D_effective > f_min where R_base alone is insufficient.
P070: Topology drift: mesh or federation gradually becoming star-adjacent through centralization pressure.
P071: 12. Conclusion
P072: The current SOE Governance Architecture is best described as a simulation-constrained layered governance control system. Its strongest result is not that it identifies one safe governance form. Its strongest result is that it shows why governance safety is topology-conditioned, monitor-dependent, and recovery-constrained. A structure that is safe under one monitoring rule may be invisible to that same rule under another topology. A recovery mechanism that works at the disturbance floor may fail under sustained external load. A governance module that exists may still be ineffective if trust has collapsed.
P073: The architecture is therefore ready for an integrated grand simulation, but not for deployment claims beyond the tested regimes. The correct next step is to simulate the whole C00-C08 stack under mixed-node, topology-changing, resource-constrained conditions and use the results to update the component specs again.
